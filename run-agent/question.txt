# Q1: substantive mathematical issue inventory

## Task

Read the whole LaTeX source of a mathematical paper, given at the end of this
message. Report every independent substantive mathematical defect in it,
following the rules below, and classify each one as `minor_issue`,
`moderate_issue`, or `serious_issue`. Review the entire paper; do not stop
after the first or most serious issue. If you find no qualifying issue, return
an empty issue list.

## Source and tags

The source is the only evidence; do not rely on anything outside it. Every
theorem-like block (theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary, claim, ...) and
every identifiable algorithm block has a unique `\label{...}`; duplicated
labels have been made unique with suffixes `-1`, `-2`, and so on. Whenever a
field asks for a tag, copy the literal label from the source; never invent one.
An equation has a tag only if it carries its own `\label{...}`; unlabelled
displays have no tag. If the source is missing, truncated, or unreadable,
return `status` `input_error`.

## What counts as an issue

One issue is one root mathematical defect together with everything that
becomes unreliable because of it. If repairing the root would also fix a
downstream claim, that claim is part of the same issue (list it under
`dependent_affected_formal_result_tags`), not a new one; report a downstream
claim separately only if it has its own defect that would survive repairing
the root. Independent defects are separate issues even when they affect the
same theorem, proof, definition, or algorithm.

Report an issue only if all of the following hold:

1. The defective claim, definition, construction, algorithmic step, or proof
   step is in the source.
2. The source gives a concrete mathematical reason that it is false,
   ill-defined, incomplete, or unsupported as written: a counterexample, a
   violated hypothesis, an invalid inference, a missing nontrivial argument,
   an incompatible definition, or a similar specific failure. Suspicion, or a
   wish for more exposition, is not enough.
3. The problem is mathematical, not stylistic or pedagogical.
4. Its effect is clear enough to establish a substantive defect. You do not
   need to know how to repair it.

Do not report routine omitted algebra or pure typos. A mistake is a pure typo
only if a competent reader can see the single forced correction directly from
the source and continue without repairing any inference, argument, or
construction. It is a mathematical issue, not a typo, if the reasoning depends
on the erroneous meaning, if a premise or inference has to be repaired, if
there is a choice between mathematical repairs, or if the correction withdraws
a substantive promise.

## Coverage

Check the main claims, every theorem-like result and its proof, definitions,
displayed claims, algorithms, and statements of scope or guarantees. Look at
hypotheses, quantifiers, parameter regimes, boundary and degenerate cases;
whether invoked results are applied within their hypotheses; whether
calculations, estimates, and case splits actually prove what is claimed;
whether algorithms are well defined and deliver their stated guarantees; and
whether the prose claims more than the formal content supports. Use the
labelled blocks as a checklist. One target can have several independent
defects.

## Target type

Assign exactly one `target_type`:

- `formal_result`: a theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary, or explicit formal
  claim whose statement or proof fails. A faulty equation or proof step inside
  such a result is a `formal_result` issue.
- `algorithm`: a procedure, pseudocode block, estimator, or executable
  construction whose operation, invariant, input-output contract, or guarantee
  fails.
- `prose_claim`: an independently false, unsupported, or materially misleading
  claim about what the paper establishes, achieves, computes, assumes, covers,
  or implies. A prose restatement of a defective formal result or algorithm is
  normally a consequence of the same root, not a second issue.
- `other`: a labelled standalone mathematical assertion fitting none of the
  above, such as a displayed equation that is itself the false claim rather
  than a proof step. Use sparingly.

The type belongs to the claim or procedure whose validity fails, not to the
definition, equation, assumption, or invoked result that merely supplies the
evidence. When one root defeats a labelled formal result, use `formal_result`.
Do not report one root twice under different types.

## Primary tag and dependent results

`primary_affected_tag` is the label of the claim or procedure whose own
validity fails, not of the evidence and not of a downstream consequence: a
faulty proof step belongs to the result being proved; a result that misapplies
another result owns the defect; a false supporting result owns the defect and
its later uses are dependents; when an argument proves less than claimed, the
stated result owns the defect.

- `formal_result`: the result's own label, required. If the result has several
  labels, use the first in source order.
- `algorithm`: the label that most narrowly identifies the failing procedure
  (an algorithm environment, or an equation or definition that is itself the
  construction). Use `null` if there is no suitable label; then
  `failure_location` must pin down the failing step.
- `other`: the assertion's own label, required.
- `prose_claim`: its own label if it has one, otherwise `null`.

Never borrow a nearby label. `dependent_affected_formal_result_tags` lists,
transitively, every labelled formal result invalidated by the same root, under
the same tag rules. Exclude mere citations, results with an independent valid
proof, and results that use only a part that remains established.

## Failure mechanism

For a `formal_result`, assign exactly one `failure_mechanism` describing the
root failure (not its repair or its effects):

- `statement_support_mismatch`: the argument is valid but establishes a
  materially weaker or different conclusion than the statement.
- `invalid_input_or_dependency`: the proof relies on an undefined object, a
  false or unestablished premise, or a result used outside its hypotheses.
  An omitted hypothesis that the proof assumes or needs without establishing
  it falls here.
- `invalid_or_incomplete_reasoning`: the inputs are legitimate but the proof's
  own inference, calculation, limit, construction, invariant, or case split
  fails, or a necessary nontrivial step is missing.
- `other`: a formal failure fitting none of the above. Use only after ruling
  the three out, and say why in `failure_explanation`; it is not a label for
  uncertainty.

For every other target type use `not_applicable`.

## Severity

Classify each issue by the least severe complete resolution the source
supports, counting the repair burden and the mathematical loss for the issue
and everything that depends on it.

- `minor_issue`: a short, local, routine repair or bypass preserves every
  substantive claim and guarantee, with no new nontrivial lemma, construction,
  reoptimization, or replacement argument. Adding a standard local condition
  or fixing a degenerate case can be minor when the method, conclusion, rate,
  and advertised scope all survive.
- `moderate_issue`: more than minor, but some supported resolution avoids new
  global proof architecture and avoids major loss. It may need a contained
  nontrivial argument, a new lemma or construction, a reoptimization, or a
  limited weakening that leaves a recognizably comparable result.
- `serious_issue`: every supported resolution needs essentially new proof
  architecture or causes major loss: a substantive result is abandoned, an
  advertised regime is excluded, a central quantifier or guarantee type
  changes, or an important rate or complexity guarantee materially worsens.
  An issue is serious even with no dependents if no comparable version of its
  primary claim survives. If the source supports no complete resolution, the
  issue is serious; a speculative repair does not lower severity.

Do not infer severity from how prominent the statement is, how many results
depend on it, or how long the edit is. A one-line correction is moderate or
serious if it necessarily loses mathematical content.
